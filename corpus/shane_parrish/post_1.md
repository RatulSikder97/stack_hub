# First-order thinking is the trap

A useful definition: first-order thinking asks what happens. Second-order thinking asks what happens after that, and what happens after that.

Most decisions in modern organizations are made at the first-order level. Cut the cost. Ship the feature. Hire the candidate. The immediate effect is visible and easy to defend. The downstream effects are not.

The problem is not that first-order thinking is wrong. It is that it is incomplete in a particular way. Second-order effects often do not arrive for months, or quarters, and by the time they arrive the original decision-maker has moved on to the next first-order win. The cost is dispersed across a system that nobody owns.

The simplest practical move I have seen work: before any non-trivial decision, ask one question. "And then what?" Repeat it three times. The third answer is usually where the actual risk is sitting.

This is not a complete theory of decision-making. It is a small reusable habit that costs nothing and corrects a recurring blind spot. It is also, in my experience, the single most reliable indicator that someone is thinking carefully versus moving fast.

Move fast where reversibility is cheap. Slow down where the third question surfaces something you cannot undo.
