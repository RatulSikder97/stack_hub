# Inversion as a thinking habit

Most people, when faced with a goal, ask how to achieve it. Inversion asks the opposite. What would guarantee failure here? Then avoid those things first.

This sounds trivial. In practice it is one of the most consistently underused tools in decision-making. The reason is that the failure cases are often more concrete than the success cases, and easier to enumerate, and therefore easier to defend against.

A worked example. A team wants to launch a new product on time. The standard plan focuses on the path to launch — milestones, owners, scope. The inverted plan focuses on what would derail the launch. Vendor delays. Founder distraction. A late-stage scope change someone snuck in on a Friday. The inverted list, written honestly, is shorter than the milestones list and predicts the actual cause of the launch slipping with surprising accuracy.

The reason inversion works is that goals are usually fuzzy and failures are usually specific. By naming the specific failures, you get a checklist your team can use. By naming only the goal, you get a wish.

The discipline is not that inversion replaces forward planning. It is that you do both, and you take the inverted list seriously when the team pushes back on what feels like negative thinking. That pushback is the exact place the project is most likely to fail.
