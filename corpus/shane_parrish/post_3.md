# Decision quality is not outcome quality

A high-quality decision can lead to a bad outcome. A low-quality decision can lead to a great outcome. The quality of the decision and the quality of the outcome are related, on average, over many decisions, but they are not the same thing.

This matters because most people, most of the time, evaluate decisions by their outcomes. If it worked, it was a smart call. If it did not, the person who made it was reckless. This is intuitive and almost always wrong, because it teaches the wrong lessons.

The way professional poker players think about it is closer to right. Separate the decision from the result. Was the input information used well? Was the probability of each outcome assessed honestly? Were the costs of being wrong sized correctly? Then look at the result, and update only on the part the decision could have controlled.

In organizations, you can build this into your retrospectives. After a decision plays out, do not just ask whether it worked. Ask whether you would make the same decision again with the information you had at the time. If yes, do not punish the team for a bad outcome. If no, study the gap. That is the only place real learning happens.

Companies that confuse the two end up rewarding luck and punishing courage. Both compound the wrong way.
