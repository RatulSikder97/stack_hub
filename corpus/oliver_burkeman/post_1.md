# The cost of keeping every option open

Most modern productivity advice treats time as a resource to be optimized. The unspoken assumption: if you become efficient enough, you can do everything that matters. The trouble is that the assumption is wrong, and quietly destructive.

You cannot do everything that matters. You will, in fact, fail to do most of it. The question is whether you choose what you fail to do, or whether you let the choice happen to you by default while you are answering email.

There is a particular flavor of exhaustion that comes from refusing to choose. It is not the exhaustion of long days; it is the exhaustion of holding too many futures open at once, in the hope that none of them will require closing. The more options you preserve, the more attention each one extracts from you, every hour, in the form of low-grade unresolved tension.

The way out is not better optimization. It is the boring practice of letting things go on purpose. Pick the work that matters this season. Tell the truth about what that costs. Allow the projects you are not doing to actually be not done, rather than perpetually almost-started.

This is not a counsel of despair. It is the most concrete form of freedom available — freedom from the silent obligation of every option you never closed.
